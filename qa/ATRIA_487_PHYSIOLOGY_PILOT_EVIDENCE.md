# ATRIA 4.8.7 shared physiology pilot: clinical evidence

Research date: **2026-10-11 UTC**. Scope: the existing oxygen, Ringer/crystalloid, norepinephrine, IV morphine and IV furosemide actions, illustrated in `PERI-SEC-001`, `HDA-001` and `CIRR-001`. This review is independent of existing simulator coefficients. It supplies qualitative expectations and documented timing, not a bedside protocol or validated patient prediction.

## Primary-source context

| Context | Evidence relevant to the pilot |
|---|---|
| Secondary peritonitis with sepsis/vasoplegia | SSC **2026**, published 23 March 2026, supports crystalloids, preferentially balanced solutions, and norepinephrine as the first vasopressor. Fluid response needs repeated assessment. Persistent hypotension can require vasopressors; unstable shock can justify concurrent crystalloid/vasopressor administration. Infection/source control remains necessary. Fluid removal belongs after acute resuscitation; escalating/high vasopressor requirements or continued volume expansion define that acute phase. [S1] |
| Upper gastrointestinal bleeding | ACG 2021 distinguishes resuscitation from endoscopic hemostasis. The guideline author's official ACG teaching material emphasizes resuscitation/comorbidities before endoscopy and notes that hemoglobin can fall after fluid equilibration. Thus pressure or saturation improvement alone cannot establish bleeding control. [S4] |
| Cirrhosis with ascites | AASLD 2021 supports spironolactone with or without furosemide for moderate ascites, and large-volume paracentesis for tense ascites. Excess total-body fluid can coexist with poor effective arterial filling. Renal function, sodium and weight matter; diagnosed AKI is a reason to stop diuretics. [S3] |

BTS's current published general acute-care oxygen guidance uses 94–98% for most patients and 88–92% for patients at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure. Its official status page retains those recommendations while a replacement guideline is under development. [S2a, S2b] **SSC 2026 is more contextual in sepsis with acute hypoxemic respiratory failure:** it permits lower/conservative or higher/liberal targets according to patient factors/resources; trial targets were approximately 90–93% versus at least 96%. Do not attribute one mandatory 94–98% target to SSC 2026. [S1]

## Qualitative expectations for independent tests

These are **clinical syntheses/inferences** from the sources, not source-backed numerical vital-sign deltas. Case IDs illustrate states; they must not substitute for the underlying state.

| Therapy | PERI-SEC: hypoperfusion/vasoplegia | HDA: bleeding/volume loss | CIRR: ascites/congestion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oxygen [S1, S2b] | Can improve hypoxemia; does not cure the infection or replace circulatory resuscitation. | Can improve hypoxemia; saturation does not restore lost hemoglobin or stop hemorrhage. | Benefit requires hypoxemia; ascites alone does not imply oxygen benefit. |
| IV balanced crystalloid [S1, S4, S5] | Can support filling/perfusion when responsive; additional fluid can accumulate without proportional pressure benefit. | Can support circulation while bleeding continues; it cannot restore red-cell mass or provide hemostasis. | Conditional benefit if underfilled; additional salt/water can aggravate congestion. |
| Norepinephrine infusion [S1, S6] | Raises vascular tone/pressure; a pressure response is not infection resolution. | Pressure can rise despite unresolved hypovolemia and impaired organ perfusion. | No routine benefit from uncomplicated ascites; a separate shock or HRS-AKI state changes its relevance. [S3] |
| IV morphine [S10] | Analgesia can coexist with lower ventilation, sedation and hypotension. | Pain relief does not stop bleeding; low circulating volume increases hypotension risk. | Analgesia is possible; cirrhosis/renal failure can prolong exposure and adverse effects. |
| IV furosemide [S1, S3, S8] | Does not rescue volume-depleted shock; later congestion after resuscitation is a different state. | Diuresis can further deplete circulating volume and worsen hypoperfusion. | Can produce natriuresis/diuresis when responsive, with risks of underfilling, electrolyte loss and AKI. |

## Documented timing, route and limitations

| Exact treatment/source | Onset and maximum/steady state | Clearance and effect duration |
|---|---|---|
| **Inhaled oxygen**, delivered continuously by an appropriate device [S2b] | BTS asks for at least **5 minutes of saturation observation** after starting, changing concentration or stopping. This is a monitoring interval, **not** a pharmacokinetic onset or half-life. | No transferable drug half-life or fixed duration is supplied. Response depends on delivered oxygen, ventilation, gas exchange and continuing delivery. |
| **Lactated Ringer's Injection USP, IV infusion**, B. Braun 1000 mL bag [S5] | Label provides no universal onset or peak for a pressure response. Infused volume/rate and the patient's condition govern its effects. | No conventional onset/peak/elimination-half-life profile is provided for this mixed electrolyte/water solution. Do not assign it norepinephrine-like clearance. |
| **Norepinephrine bitartrate premix, continuous IV infusion**, Baxter, norepinephrine-base equivalent 16/32/64 micrograms/mL in 5% dextrose or 0.9% sodium chloride [S6] | Pressor response is rapid; pressor and plasma steady state are reached within approximately **5 minutes**. | Mean plasma half-life approximately **2.4 minutes**; mean metabolic clearance **3.1 L/min**. Label states pressor action ends **1–2 minutes after infusion stops**. These describe different endpoints and are not interchangeable decay constants. |
| **Morphine sulfate preservative-free solution, IV**, 4/8/10 mg/mL single-dose vials, US label [S7] | Exact IV analgesic onset/duration are not supplied in this selected label. Maximum **CNS effect can be delayed approximately 30 minutes**. Supplemental manufacturer information for **DBL morphine sulfate pentahydrate 10 mg/mL, slow IV** reports peak **analgesic** effect within **20 minutes** [S9]; endpoint/formulation descriptions differ. | US label: accepted elimination half-life in normal subjects **1.5–2 hours**, with reported terminal estimates **1.5–4.5 hours**; postoperative plasma clearance **0.9–1.2 L/kg/hour**, with considerable variation. This is not a fixed analgesic duration. |
| **Furosemide Injection USP 10 mg/mL, IV**, conventional injection [S8] | Diuresis begins within **5 minutes**; peak diuretic effect occurs within the first **30 minutes**. Do not use oral or subcutaneous product kinetics. | Diuretic effect approximately **2 hours**; terminal half-life approximately **2 hours**. Predominantly excreted unchanged in urine. These times concern drug/effect activity, not reversal of net fluid loss. |

**Morphine route trap:** another current IV/IM label places **5–20-minute onset, 60-minute peak and 3–4-hour duration** in its **IM** efficacy discussion. Those numbers must not be relabelled as IV values. [S10]

## Organ-function modifiers and safety directions

- **Morphine:** cirrhosis lowers clearance and increases half-life. Renal failure raises exposure, decreases clearance and permits M3G/M6G accumulation. The US label does not establish precise multipliers for severe impairment. It warns of respiratory depression and severe hypotension, including circulatory shock. [S10]
- **Furosemide:** anuria is a contraindication; renal disease and hypoproteinemia can reduce useful response and increase toxicity risk. Excess diuresis can cause blood-volume depletion/circulatory collapse. Abrupt fluid/electrolyte shifts in cirrhosis can precipitate hepatic coma. No single hepatic/renal half-life multiplier is justified by this label. [S8]
- **Lactated Ringer's:** the selected current label warns against use in severe renal impairment because of hyperkalemia/overload, and in severe hepatic impairment because lactate metabolism may fail. A cirrhosis diagnosis alone is not evidence that every patient has severe hepatic impairment. This source applies to **lactated** Ringer/Hartmann solution, not an unspecified Ringer formulation. [S5]
- **Norepinephrine:** the label does not quantify a renal/hepatic dose-to-clearance correction. In hypovolemia it can produce apparently normal pressure with reduced renal/systemic flow, tissue hypoxia and lactic acidosis. Hepatic/other-tissue metabolism and urinary metabolite excretion do not justify an invented organ-failure multiplier. [S6]

## Simulator calibration: explicitly engineering choices

The sources above do **not** validate fixed millimeters-of-mercury changes, per-case drug multipliers, universal fluid-overload thresholds, overdose cutoffs, severity bins or a mathematical response curve.

1. Model delivered treatment and state: amount, route, elapsed simulated time, infusion/delivery status, oxygen target, volume responsiveness, congestion, organ function and prior exposure. The same state should produce the same response regardless of case ID.
2. Continuing oxygen and norepinephrine delivery should sustain an exposure-dependent effect. Norepinephrine cessation should permit rapid loss of support; it should not behave like an hours-long single bolus.
3. Track fluid input, redistribution and output separately from drug exposure. A fluid bolus need not improve every pressure reading; its administered water must not vanish when a pharmacology timer ends.
4. Furosemide activity can decline while the resulting fluid deficit persists. Increased urine production is not instant clearance of ascites or recovery from AKI. AASLD's ascites response is assessed through weight/sodium/renal monitoring, not a rapid vital-sign reset. [S3]
5. Preserve morphine's delayed/overlapping effects after repeated administration. Analgesia, sedation, respiratory drive and arterial pressure are separate outcomes. Supplemental oxygen can sustain saturation without reversing opioid respiratory depression. [S2b, S7]
6. Make benefit, absent benefit and harm conditional on the current state. Congestion and underfilling can coexist; a rising pressure or saturation alone does not prove adequate perfusion or etiologic recovery.
7. Numerical tuning should be labelled **simulator calibration** and verified with bounds, monotonicity/saturation, cumulative exposure, cessation, competing treatments and state-matched cross-case comparisons. Preserve uncertainty instead of presenting selected label averages as universal patient constants.
